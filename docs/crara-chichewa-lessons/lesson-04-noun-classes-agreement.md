# Chichewa — Lesson 4: Noun classes and agreement

**Level:** Beginner to lower-intermediate  
**Learning goal:** Recognise common singular/plural noun patterns and see how agreement changes across a noun phrase.

## 1. What is a noun class?

In Chichewa, nouns belong to grammatical classes. A class often has a characteristic prefix, and many classes have a singular/plural partner. The class matters because other words in a sentence may agree with the noun.

Do not assume that every noun forms its plural by adding one universal ending. Learn nouns together with their class and a reliable plural form.

## 2. Common singular and plural pairs

| Singular | Plural | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| munthu | anthu | person / people |
| mwana | ana | child / children |
| chinthu | zinthu | thing / things |
| buku | mabuku | book / books |

The beginnings of these nouns change in different ways. These are learning pairs, not a rule that can be applied to every noun.

## 3. Agreement in descriptions

The describing word changes to agree with the noun. Compare:

| Singular noun phrase | Plural noun phrase | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| munthu wabwino | anthu abwino | a good person / good people |
| mwana wabwino | ana abwino | a good child / good children |
| chinthu chabwino | zinthu zabwino | a good thing / good things |
| buku labwino | mabuku abwino | a good book / good books |

The forms visible in these examples are part of the agreement patterns associated with the noun classes; they are not interchangeable.

## 4. Agreement in simple statements

A noun's class can also affect the subject marker used with a verb. Compare these introductory examples:

- **Munthu akudya.** — The person is eating.
- **Anthu akudya.** — The people are eating.
- **Chinthu chikugwa.** — The thing is falling.
- **Zinthu zikugwa.** — The things are falling.

In the first two examples, the human singular and plural forms use **a-** in this verb pattern. In the latter two, the class 7/8 subject markers are **chi-** and **zi-**. This is an introduction, not a complete subject-marker chart.

## 5. How to study a new noun

When CRARA introduces a noun, it should where possible teach:

1. the singular form;
2. the plural form;
3. the English meaning;
4. the noun class or class pairing, when verified;
5. one agreeing description;
6. one short sentence showing the noun in context.

This prevents learners from memorising an isolated word without learning how to use it.

## 6. Guided practice

1. What is the plural of **munthu**?
2. What is the plural of **mwana**?
3. What is the singular of **zinthu**?
4. Complete the pair: **buku / ______**.
5. Which phrase means “good people”: **munthu wabwino** or **anthu abwino**?
6. Which phrase means “good books”: **buku labwino** or **mabuku abwino**?
7. In **Chinthu chikugwa**, identify the noun and the subject marker.

### Answer key

1. anthu
2. ana
3. chinthu
4. mabuku
5. anthu abwino
6. mabuku abwino
7. **Chinthu** is the noun; **chi-** is the subject marker in **chikugwa**.

## 7. Teaching and source-quality note

The supplied February 2011 guide introduces noun prefixes and agreement, but its examples and spelling conventions are not consistent enough to serve as a complete modern noun-class reference. This lesson uses a small set of familiar illustrative forms and does not claim to list every noun class or every concord pattern.

CRARA must not:
- assign a noun class solely by guessing from its first letter;
- invent plural forms by applying one pattern to every noun;
- assume adjective and verb agreement use identical prefixes;
- treat this introductory set as a complete noun-class table.

For formal secondary-school teaching, extend this lesson using the retrieved MIE Forms 1–2 and Forms 3–4 syllabuses, and verify detailed concord paradigms against a reliable contemporary grammar or a fluent Malawian Chichewa speaker.
