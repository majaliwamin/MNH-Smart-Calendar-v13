# Chichewa — Lesson 7: Possession and “my child”

**Level:** Beginner to lower-intermediate  
**Learning goal:** Recognise a basic possessive expression and understand why possessive forms must be learned with the noun they accompany.

## 1. What does possession express?

A possessive expression shows a relationship such as ownership, belonging or association. In English, examples include “my book”, “your child” and “our home”.

In Chichewa, do not assume that one possessive form can be attached to every noun. The form may be affected by the noun class and the grammatical construction.

## 2. A source-supported example

The supplied February 2011 *Chichewa from English* guide gives this example in its brief grammar section:

- **mwana wanga** — “my child”

The guide prints the phrase inside a longer sentence as **Ndi maw-na WANGA**, glossed there as “He is MY child.” The spacing and spelling in that sentence are visibly inconsistent. This lesson therefore uses the clearly recognisable phrase **mwana wanga** as an introductory example, while preserving the guide's original sentence only as a source transcription.

Notice the two parts:

- **mwana** — child
- **wanga** — my (in this example)

Learn the complete phrase together. Do not detach **wanga** and apply it to every noun.

## 3. What this example does — and does not — teach

The example introduces one possessive relationship. It does not provide a complete table for:

- my / your / his or her;
- our / your (plural or respectful) / their;
- all singular and plural noun classes;
- every form of “mine” or “belonging to”.

Possessive concord is connected to noun class. For that reason, CRARA must verify each additional form with a reliable contemporary grammar or fluent Malawian Chichewa speaker before presenting it as a general rule.

## 4. Learn possession with noun-class agreement

You have already learned that noun classes affect forms in noun phrases:

- **mwana wabwino** — a good child
- **ana abwino** — good children

Possessive expressions also need to be learned in context. When a new example is introduced, record the noun, its class or class pairing where verified, the possessive form, and the complete meaning. Do not infer the possessive form from the adjective pattern.

## 5. Guided practice

1. What does **mwana** mean?
2. What does **wanga** mean in the example **mwana wanga**?
3. Translate **mwana wanga**.
4. Why should **wanga** not automatically be used with every noun?
5. Does the supplied guide establish a complete table for “my, your, his/her, our, your (plural), their”?
6. What should CRARA do before teaching additional possessive forms?

### Answer key

1. Child.
2. My, in this example.
3. My child.
4. Possessive forms are affected by grammatical agreement; this one example is not a universal pattern.
5. No.
6. Verify the form and its noun-class agreement using a reliable contemporary grammar or a fluent Malawian Chichewa speaker.

## 6. Teaching and source-quality note

This lesson uses the possession example in the user's supplied February 2011 guide, page 38. The guide itself labels its brief grammar as a challenge to study and discuss with a natural speaker. Its sentence has inconsistent spacing and spelling. Preserve that transcription when explaining the document, but do not teach the malformed sentence as a clean model.

CRARA must not:
- claim that **wanga** is a universal possessive form for every noun;
- invent a complete possessive paradigm from the single example;
- assume possessive agreement follows the same pattern as adjective agreement;
- silently repair the source's printed sentence and present the repair as a direct quotation;
- invent forms for “your”, “his/her”, “our” or “their” without verification.

**Next:** extend possessives only after contemporary verification, then move to adjectives and modifiers with noun-class agreement.
