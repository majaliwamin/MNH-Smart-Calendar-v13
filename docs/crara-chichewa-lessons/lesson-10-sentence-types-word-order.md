# Lesson 10: Sentence types and careful word-order analysis

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to distinguish a statement, a question, a command and a negative sentence; identify a verb phrase in a simple example; and explain why sentence patterns must be learned from reliable examples rather than guessed from English word order.

## 1. Four common sentence purposes

Sentences can be grouped by what a speaker is doing:

| Sentence type | Purpose | English example |
|---|---|---|
| Statement (declarative) | Gives information | The student is reading. |
| Question (interrogative) | Asks for information | Who is speaking? |
| Command (imperative) | Tells or asks someone to do something | Please listen. |
| Negative | Says that something is not the case | The student is not reading. |

A negative sentence is often described by its grammatical polarity rather than as a separate sentence purpose: a question or command can also be negative. These labels describe meaning and function; they do not by themselves tell us which Chichewa prefixes or particles to use.

## 2. Recognising the verb phrase

A verb expresses an action, event or state. In the supplied February 2011 guide, the following printed examples are glossed as shown:

| Chichewa as printed | Guide's English gloss |
|---|---|
| **Ndi ku pita** | I am going |
| **Ndi ku dya** | I am eating |
| **Mu ku yankhula** | You are talking |
| **A ku pita** | They are going |
| **Ndi-ku-funa ku-gula pwhetekele** | I want to buy tomatoes |

The guide varies its hyphenation and spacing. These examples are retained as source transcriptions, not silently standardised spellings.

In the last example, the guide glosses the whole expression as “I want to buy tomatoes”. It is useful for noticing that a sentence can contain more than one verb-related element. Do not use this one example to claim a complete rule for infinitives, object placement or all sentence word order.

## 3. Questions

Lesson 6 introduced question words listed in the same guide:

- **ndani?** — who?
- **chani?** — what?
- **kuti?** — where?
- **chifukwa?** — why?
- **zingati?** — how much/how many, depending on context

A question word helps identify the information being requested. For example, “who?” asks about a person, while “where?” asks about a place. This lesson does not add new Chichewa question sentences because the source's question examples include inconsistent or mismatched material. Reuse the carefully documented vocabulary, and verify a complete question pattern before teaching it as a model.

## 4. Commands and negative forms

The 2011 guide contains sections on imperatives and negatives, but its examples and explanations need contemporary grammatical checking. For that reason, this lesson introduces the concepts without manufacturing Chichewa forms:

- An **imperative** is used to give an instruction, request or command.
- A **negative construction** expresses that an action or state does not happen, did not happen, or should not happen.
- The exact Chichewa form may depend on the verb, subject, tense/aspect and sentence context.

Do not create a negative or imperative by simply adding an English equivalent of “not” or by copying a prefix from a different verb pattern. Confirm each pattern against a reliable contemporary grammar and fluent Malawian usage.

## 5. Word order: analyse, do not guess

English commonly uses subject–verb–object order in simple active statements, as in “The learner (subject) reads (verb) a book (object).” This English example is only a way to identify grammatical roles.

Chichewa word order and agreement must be taught from verified Chichewa examples. The limited examples in the supplied 2011 guide are not enough to establish a complete word-order rule. In particular:

- Do not assume that Chichewa always follows English word order.
- Do not infer a subject, object or agreement marker from a prefix alone without checking the whole construction.
- Distinguish a subject marker within a verb from an independent pronoun.
- Treat the guide's printed spacing and hyphenation as source features, not as a consistent modern orthographic standard.
