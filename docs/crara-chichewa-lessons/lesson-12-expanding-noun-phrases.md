# Chichewa — Lesson 12: Expanding noun phrases with modifiers

**Level:** Beginner to lower-intermediate  
**Learning goal:** Recognise how a noun phrase can identify, describe or show a relationship to a person or thing, while keeping each modifier attached to a verified example.

## 1. What is a noun phrase?

A noun phrase is a group of words organised around a noun. The noun names a person, animal, place or thing. Other words may help identify it, describe it or show a relationship to it.

In English, compare:
- child;
- this child;
- my child;
- a good child.

Chichewa also uses modifiers in noun phrases. Their forms and placement are grammatical, however, and must be learned from reliable examples rather than translated word-for-word from English.

## 2. Identify three kinds of modifier in the examples already studied

This lesson brings together three small sets from earlier lessons. Learn each complete phrase as documented.

### A. Demonstrative: identifying or pointing

Lesson 5 records:

- **mwana uyu** — this child
- **ichi** — this thing (the guide's gloss)
- **icho** — that thing (the guide's gloss)

The guide also gives **Mwana uyu ali pano.** — “This child is here.”

The forms **ichi** and **icho** are shown as standalone examples in the guide. Do not assume they can be placed after every noun. The guide's distance explanations are inconsistent, so these examples do not establish a complete near/far system.

### B. Possessive: showing a relationship

Lesson 7 records one clearly recognisable phrase:

- **mwana wanga** — my child

In this example, **mwana** means “child” and **wanga** expresses “my”. Learn the complete phrase. The source does not provide a complete possessive table, and **wanga** must not be applied to every noun.

### C. Descriptive modifier: giving a quality

Lesson 8 records these noun phrases:

| Chichewa | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **mwana wabwino** | a good child |
| **ana abwino** | good children |
| **munthu wabwino** | a good person |
| **anthu abwino** | good people |
| **chinthu chabwino** | a good thing |
| **zinthu zabwino** | good things |
| **buku labwino** | a good book |
| **mabuku abwino** | good books |

The descriptive form changes in these examples. This is a limited learning set, not a universal adjective formula.

## 3. Compare the functions

| Modifier type | Documented phrase | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Demonstrative | **mwana uyu** | Identifies/points to this child |
| Possessive | **mwana wanga** | Shows the “my” relationship |
| Descriptive | **mwana wabwino** | Describes the child as good |

Notice that the same noun **mwana** appears in all three examples. The modifier has a different function in each phrase.

## 4. A crucial rule: do not combine forms by guesswork

The examples above are individually documented. They do **not** prove that any two or three modifiers can be combined in any order.

For example, this lesson does not introduce combined forms such as “this good child” or “my good child” in Chichewa. Before teaching such a phrase, CRARA must verify:
- whether the proposed demonstrative or possessive form agrees with the noun;
- the correct order of the modifiers;
- whether the complete phrase is idiomatic in contemporary Malawian Chichewa.

Do not create a new phrase by simply joining **mwana uyu**, **mwana wanga** and **mwana wabwino**. A fluent-speaker or reliable contemporary grammar check is required.

## 5. A method for analysing an expanded noun phrase

When you encounter a phrase:

1. Find the head noun and identify its meaning.
2. Identify the modifier: is it pointing, showing possession or describing?
3. Read the complete phrase, not just the modifier in isolation.
4. Check whether the agreement and word order are supported by a reliable example.
5. Record the source and avoid extending the pattern to other nouns without verification.
