# Chichewa — Lesson 13: Verb structure and subject agreement

**Level:** Beginner to lower-intermediate  
**Learning goal:** Recognise the verb phrase in familiar sentences, compare source-documented subject-related forms, and observe how tense/aspect patterns are expressed in a small set of examples.

## 1. What does a verb do?

A verb expresses an action, event or state. Examples in the supplied guide include forms glossed as “go”, “eat”, “speak”, “come” and “sleep”.

The guide prints these infinitives:

| English gloss in the guide | Form printed |
|---|---|
| go | **ku-pita** |
| eat | **ku-dya** |
| speak | **ku-lankhula** |
| come | **ku-bwera** |
| sleep | **ku-gona** |

These are transcriptions of the 2011 guide, not a complete verb dictionary or a standardised spelling table.

## 2. A verb phrase can contain more than the verb root

The guide's grammar notes explain that verb forms may contain different grammatical parts, including prefixes, infixes and suffixes. Its examples include:

- **Ndi-ku-dya** — “I am eating” (subject-related form and activity pattern in the guide).
- **Ti-ma-pita** — “We go” (habitual pattern).
- **Ti-na-pita** — “We went” (past pattern).
- **Ti-dza-pita** — “We will go” (future pattern).

The guide varies spaces and hyphens between sections. Keep these forms identified as printed source examples; do not silently standardise them or treat this small set as a full conjugation table.

## 3. Compare the documented time/aspect patterns

The source uses the same verb **-pita** (“go”) to illustrate a contrast:

| Meaning described by the guide | Form printed |
|---|---|
| We are going (present activity) | **Ti-ku-pita** |
| We go (habitual) | **Ti-ma-pita** |
| We went (past) | **Ti-na-pita** |
| We will go (future) | **Ti-dza-pita** |

Observe that the part following **Ti-** changes across these four examples. In this set, the guide associates those forms with different time/aspect meanings.

This is a comparison of the source's examples, not permission to attach **-ku-**, **-ma-**, **-na-** or **-dza-** to any verb or subject without checking. Contemporary Chichewa grammar may describe the morphology and terminology more precisely than this introductory guide.

## 4. Subject agreement: compare complete examples

Lesson 4 introduced these sentences:

| Source example | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Munthu akudya.** | The person is eating. |
| **Anthu akudya.** | The people are eating. |
| **Chinthu chikugwa.** | The thing is falling. |
| **Zinthu zikugwa.** | The things are falling. |

Compare the noun and the verb together:

- **Munthu / Anthu** are the human singular/plural pair in this example set.
- **Chinthu / Zinthu** are the thing/things pair.
- The verb forms shown are **akudya**, **chikugwa** and **zikugwa**.

The earlier lesson identifies **chi-** and **zi-** as the subject markers in **chikugwa** and **zikugwa**. It treats **a-** in the human examples as the marker used in that particular pattern. Do not turn these observations into a complete subject-marker chart.

A useful rule for learners: identify the subject noun and read the whole verb form before explaining agreement. A prefix seen in isolation may have different functions in another construction.

## 5. Independent pronouns are not the same as subject markers

Lesson 5 distinguished independent pronouns such as **ine** (“I/me”) and **ife** (“we/us”) from short grammatical forms used with verbs.

Do not assume that an independent pronoun and a subject marker are interchangeable words. The source examples above are complete verb phrases; learn them in context rather than replacing one part with a pronoun by guesswork.

## 6. A safe method for analysing a verb

When you meet a new sentence:

1. Identify the subject noun or independent pronoun, if one is present.
2. Locate the complete verb form.
3. Compare the verb with a reliable example using the same subject and meaning.
4. Identify a subject-related marker or tense/aspect element only when the analysis is supported.
5. Record the complete sentence, its meaning and the source.
6. Do not generate a new form by attaching a familiar marker to an unfamiliar verb.

## 8. Source and accuracy note

This lesson consolidates the user's supplied February 2011 *Chichewa from English* guide, especially pages 24 and 40–42, together with the already curated CRARA Lessons 3 and 4. The guide varies spelling, spacing and hyphenation and gives introductory descriptions that should not be treated as a complete modern morphology reference.

CRARA must not:
- present the source's four **-pita** forms as a complete tense/aspect paradigm;
- generate conjugations for other verbs by analogy;
- infer every subject marker from a noun's first letter;
- confuse independent pronouns with subject markers;
- claim that all verb prefixes, infixes or suffixes have been covered;
- silently rewrite source transcriptions as though the new spelling appeared in the PDF.

For additional subjects, tenses, aspects, negatives and verb classes, verify the complete construction using a reliable contemporary Chichewa grammar and a fluent Malawian Chichewa speaker.
